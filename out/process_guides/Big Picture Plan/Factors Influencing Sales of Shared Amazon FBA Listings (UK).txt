Factors Influencing Sales of Shared Amazon FBA
Listings (UK)
When  multiple  sellers  share  the  same  Amazon  listing  (ASIN)  on  Amazon.co.uk,  various  factors
determine which seller wins the sale and how well the product sells overall. Below we examine each
measurable factor – excluding advertising – that impacts sales for shared FBA listings, explaining its
effect on sales, how it influences buyer behavior , and how to observe or measure it via Amazon’s
website or the Selling Partner API (SP-API). UK-specific considerations (like regional fulfillment and VAT-
inclusive pricing) are noted where relevant.
1. Number of Competing Sellers on the Listing
For a given product listing, the number of third-party sellers offering the item can directly affect each
seller’s sales. Generally, more competing sellers means more competition per sale : customers have more
choices, and sellers must compete on price and service to win orders. This often leads to a “race to the
bottom”  on price, as sellers undercut each other to win the Buy Box (the Featured Offer ). Each additional
seller can shrink an individual seller’s share of sales if all else is equal, since only one seller can be the
default Buy Box offer at a time.
Impact on Sales:  When only one seller is on a listing, that seller typically gets 100% of the sales (they
hold the Buy Box exclusively). With multiple sellers, Amazon will usually rotate the Buy Box among them
if they are all competitively matched, dividing the sales. For example, if 4 sellers have similar price,
Prime status, and metrics, each might get roughly 25% of the Buy Box exposure (and thus ~25% of the
sales). In practice, the split may not be equal – a seller with a better offer (lower price, faster shipping,
higher feedback) can capture a larger share, sometimes over 90% of sales  on that listing, leaving little
for others (as experienced sellers warn). More sellers also tend to drive lower prices  as they compete;
new entrants often drop the price just to grab sales, forcing others’ auto-repricing tools into continual
price cuts. This increased competition can erode profit margins and, if unchecked, lower the overall
price point of the item in the marketplace. However , a lower price can stimulate higher unit sales
volume if demand is elastic, so the listing’s total sales might grow even though each seller’s slice is
smaller .
Influence on Buyer Behavior:  Buyers on Amazon mostly just see the Featured Offer (Buy Box) when
they click “Add to Basket/Buy Now,” and many may not even realize multiple sellers are present unless
they click “See all buying options.” This means buyers typically purchase from whoever holds the Buy Box.  If
many sellers are offering the same product, buyers benefit from potentially lower prices and more
availability. They might use the “Other Sellers” section only to find a better price or shipping option if
the default offer doesn’t suit them. In general, multiple sellers drive the price toward what buyers
perceive as a fair deal (the lowest delivered price), since an overpriced seller will lose the Buy Box to a
cheaper competitor . Additionally, if one seller runs out of stock (see Stock Availability  below), buyers
seamlessly buy from the next seller in line – the overall listing remains in stock as long as someone  has
inventory. Thus, a listing with many sellers often has more consistent availability for buyers. UK buyers
specifically may also check seller location or delivery times: for example, one seller might ship from
within the UK (faster delivery) versus another shipping from overseas. In such cases, even if multiple
sellers exist, a UK-based FBA seller will have a delivery speed advantage that influences buyer choice
(customers prefer quicker local delivery and the Prime badge).

Observation/Measurement:  On Amazon’s website, you can see the number of sellers  on a listing by
the link near the price that says, for example, “ X new (and Y used) from £__ .” Clicking this shows all
offers. This number is a direct indicator of competition. Tools like Keepa or CamelCamelCamel also track
the offer count over time , which can hint at how competition is increasing or decreasing on that ASIN. 
On Amazon.co.uk:  The product detail page displays “ Other sellers on Amazon ” or “ # new from
£Price,” which tells you how many active offers exist. For instance, a listing might show “5 new
from £19.99,” indicating 5 sellers compete on that new product. Buyers can click this to compare
offers, but typically only the top offer (Buy Box) gets the sale unless the buyer actively switches. 
Using SP-API:  Developers can retrieve offer data via endpoints like the Product Pricing API . For
example, the getCompetitivePricing  or getItemOffers  endpoints can return details on
each offer  for an ASIN, including price and seller information. By counting the offers returned
(or by fields like NumberOfOffers  in the response), one can programmatically determine how
many sellers are on the listing. The SP-API’s Catalog Items API can also list the ASIN’s offers and
even differentiate by fulfillment type (FBA vs FBM), which helps measure competition. Sellers
might also track their Buy Box ownership  relative to the number of competitors (see next
section) – for instance, if you have 1 out of 5 sellers, ideally you’d aim for ~20% Buy Box win rate
if evenly matched. (In practice, win rate will depend on price and other factors.)
2. Buy Box Win Rate and Rotation
The Buy Box  (now often called the Featured Offer ) is the box on the product page with the “Buy Now”
and “Add to Basket” buttons. Winning the Buy Box is critical: by some estimates  over 80% of all
Amazon sales go through the Buy Box  (and an even higher share on mobile). When multiple sellers
are on a listing, only one gets displayed as the default seller – but Amazon will  rotate  the Buy Box
between sellers who meet its eligibility criteria. The Buy Box win rate  (or Buy Box %) is the proportion of
page views or time that your offer is the featured one. This metric directly correlates with sales volume:
if your win rate doubles, your sales should roughly double on that listing, since most buyers purchase
from the featured offer .
Impact on Sales:  A higher Buy Box win rate means greater visibility and more sales . If you are rarely
in the Buy Box, your sales will be minimal – shoppers won’t see your offer by default. In fact, for
competitive listings, being out of the Buy Box can mean “almost no sales at all” . For example, if there are
two sellers and you win the Buy Box 50% of the time, you might get roughly half the sales. If your share
drops to 10%, your sales will likely plummet accordingly. Amazon’s algorithm rotates the Buy Box based
on factors like price, shipping speed, stock, and seller performance. In an ideal scenario with all things
equal, rotation can be relatively even. Amazon notes that if, say, 4 sellers are equally  qualified and price-
matched,  each  might  get  ~25%  of  the  exposure  (the  Buy  Box  rotating  among  them  periodically).
However , if one seller has a slight edge (e.g. a lower price by a few pence or a Prime offer vs. standard
shipping), the algorithm may favor that seller more often, giving them a larger slice of the sales. In
practice, it’s common for one FBA seller with superior metrics or price to capture the majority of the Buy
Box time. Thus,  Buy Box percentage is often the single biggest driver of sales for a seller  on a
shared listing – improving it (by adjusting price or service) translates directly into selling more units.
Mechanism  (Buyer  Behavior):  Amazon  customers  usually  buy  from  the  Buy  Box  offer  without
deliberation – it’s a one-click purchase experience that Amazon optimizes for conversion. Shoppers trust
Amazon to have already sorted the “best” offer into the Buy Box. The Buy Box winner is selected by
Amazon’s  algorithm  in  real-time  based  on  multiple  criteria.  Key  factors  include:  price  (including
shipping) ,  fulfillment method  and speed (Prime eligibility),  stock availability ,  seller performance
metrics  (order defect rate, late shipments, etc.), and sometimes seller feedback rating. The result is that
buyers are more likely to get a well-priced, Prime-eligible offer with a reliable seller in the Buy Box.•

Amazon will periodically rotate who holds the Buy Box to give other eligible sellers a chance, especially
if they are very close in offer quality. This rotation can change minute by minute. For buyers, this is
invisible – they just see one offer unless they choose to look at others. If you maintain a high Buy Box
win rate, buyers effectively always see your  offer first, and thus you get the sale the majority of the time.
A low win rate means buyers usually see someone else’s offer – you only get a sale when that other
seller is out-of-stock or when Amazon rotates to you occasionally. Notably, around 80-90% of buyers
purchase through the Buy Box  rather than clicking the “other sellers” list. This means if you are not the
featured offer , you’re competing for a very small minority of more price-conscious or savvy buyers who
check all offers. In summary, winning the Buy Box  equates to visibility and convenience for the
buyer , heavily influencing purchasing behavior .
Observation/Measurement:  Amazon sellers can monitor their Buy Box win rate as a performance
metric.  Seller  Central  provides  a  “Buy  Box  %”  for  each  SKU  (in  Business  Reports  or  Inventory
Performance dashboards), which reflects the percentage of page views where your offer was the Buy
Box. A high percentage indicates you usually are the featured seller for that product. 
On Amazon’s website:  You can manually check who has the Buy Box at any given time by looking
at the product page (“ Dispatched from and sold by ... ” in the UK site’s Buy Box area). However ,
because rotation can be dynamic, a single manual check is just a snapshot. Some sellers refresh
the page or check at different times of day to see if the Buy Box switches to another seller . If you
are the seller , one simple indicator is whether your seller name is shown as the default seller on
the detail page when you view it. Additionally, buyer-facing tools like Keepa can sometimes show
Buy Box price history, which indirectly reflects if different sellers had it (e.g., a price change may
indicate a different seller’s offer became the featured one). 
Using SP-API:  There isn’t a direct “Buy Box percentage” API call, but sellers can use Business
Reports via the SP-API  to programmatically retrieve the Buy Box win rate for their products (the
Selling Partner API offers report endpoints that include the same data as the “Detail Page Sales
and Traffic” report, which has Buy Box %). Alternatively, one can periodically use the Catalog
Pricing API ( getItemOffers ) to see which seller’s offer is currently flagged as the Featured
Offer . By polling at intervals, you could estimate rotation (though this is somewhat heavy-
handed). Some third-party services also use Amazon’s SP-API or scraping to alert you when you
lose the Buy Box. The key measurable is the Buy Box owner  at a point in time (which offer has
the “Featured” flag) and over time what percentage of checks you are that owner . Amazon’s SP-
API for Catalog Items can also return the Buy Box Price  and corresponding seller ID for an ASIN,
which helps identify if you or a competitor is winning at that moment. For an official metric,
using the reports Amazon provides is most straightforward: the “By ASIN” business report shows
your Buy Box win %. This can be pulled via SP-API reports to monitor trends (e.g., see if your win
rate improves after a price change).

competition levels and prices are local. One UK-specific nuance is that all prices already include VAT –

aren’t featured until they establish some sales history. All these ensure UK shoppers get a competitive

3. Price Competition and Pricing Changes
Price  is one of the most crucial factors on Amazon. In a shared listing scenario, all sellers are usually
offering the same product, so  price becomes a primary differentiator  (aside from fulfillment). The
product’s price directly affects its attractiveness to buyers, its likelihood of winning the Buy Box, and the
volume of units sold. Small pricing changes can have a notable impact on sales volume on Amazon’s
platform, and sellers often engage in continuous price competition.
Impact on Sales:  Generally, a lower price  (all else being equal) will increase sales volume  on Amazon,
while a higher price will reduce it. This is both because of consumer choice and Amazon’s Buy Box
algorithm. If your price is lower than competitors’, you’re more likely to win the Buy Box (Amazon
heavily weighs landed price in its Buy Box decision), and buyers are more likely to purchase because
they see a better deal. Conversely, if your price is even slightly higher than another Prime seller’s, you
may lose the Buy Box and see a sharp drop in sales. For example, if competitors cut their price, you may
see your sales slow down as customers gravitate to the cheaper offer . In highly elastic markets, a small
price  drop  can  lead  to  a  proportionally  larger  increase  in  units  sold,  potentially  increasing  overall
revenue (this is classic price elasticity). However , price cuts also erode profit per unit, so sellers must find
the sweet spot. Aggressive price competition among many sellers can drive the price  down to near
break-even levels , boosting unit sales but squeezing margins for everyone. On the flip side, raising the
price  can increase profit per sale but usually  dampens sales volume  – if you raise your price above
competitors or what customers expect, your product may sell much slower or not at all if the Buy Box is
lost. There are cases where a price increase has little effect (if you were underpriced or if competitors
also  raised  prices  due  to  stock  shortages),  but  generally  Amazon  buyers  are  very  price-sensitive.
Notably, Amazon may suppress the Buy Box  (showing “See All Buying Options” instead of an add-to-
cart button) if it deems the price too high or uncompetitive – this kills sales until the price is lowered.
Thus, keeping the price competitive is vital to maintain sales velocity.
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Amazon shoppers routinely compare prices, either by looking at the Buy
Box  price  versus  list  price,  or  by  seeing  multiple  seller  offers.  The  platform  also  often  shows  a
strikethrough list price or “You Save £X” which highlights a good deal. Buyers are highly motivated by
getting the lowest price for the same item, especially on a marketplace where multiple offers are a click
away. If one seller is a few pounds cheaper , buyers will often choose that one – and Amazon’s Buy Box
will facilitate this by featuring the lowest-priced eligible  offer . In the UK, where prices include VAT, buyers
simply compare final prices (including tax and shipping). A small difference in price can shift buyer
preference unless offset by other factors (like shipping time or seller ratings). Additionally,  Amazon’s
algorithmic pricing and deal features  encourage sales when prices drop: for example, a product
might  get  a  “Limited  Time  Deal”  badge  or  simply  rank  higher  on  deal  pages  if  the  price  is  cut
significantly.  Buyers  respond  to  these  signals  with  increased  conversion  rates.  There’s  also  a
psychological aspect – prices ending in .99 or .95 can slightly improve conversion (psychological
pricing making an item appear cheaper). From a Buy Box perspective,  price (with shipping) is the
number one factor : even a difference of a few pence can decide Buy Box rotation in a tight race.
Importantly, Amazon also monitors external prices ; if your Amazon price is much higher than on other
websites, Amazon may suppress your offer (to maintain customer trust in Amazon for good deals). In
the UK, this means sellers have to be mindful of price parity (including VAT) with other retail channels.
Overall, buyers on Amazon are trained to expect competitive pricing – if your price isn’t right, many will
simply not click your listing (or will abandon the page for a cheaper alternative). Conversely, a smart
price reduction (such as during peak season or to match a competitor) can  attract bargain-seeking
buyers  and increase your conversion rate.

Observation/Measurement:  Price is straightforward to observe and adjust on Amazon. Sellers often
experiment with pricing and watch the resulting change in sales. Key metrics like unit sales, revenue,
and conversion rate will reflect the impact of price changes.
On Amazon’s site:  You can see the current Buy Box price  on the product page (and it will note if
shipping is extra or free). By clicking “Other sellers on Amazon,” you can view all prices other
sellers are charging. Historical price tracking tools (e.g., Keepa) show price history graphs, which
sometimes can be correlated with sales rank changes – for instance, if a price drop leads to a
better (lower) Best Sellers Rank, it suggests higher sales volume at that time. For a more direct
measure, sellers look at their conversion rate  on the listing (shown in Business Reports as units
ordered per sessions). If a price change occurs, one can observe the change in conversion rate
or total units sold before vs. after . A sudden competitor price drop can also be observed: if you
lose the Buy Box and your sales fall, checking the product page may reveal a new lower price
from another seller . Buyers’ behavior (like selecting filters) can also be noted – e.g., many buyers
filter search results by price or sort by price low-to-high, which means if your offer isn’t among
the cheapest, those buyers might not see it. 
Using SP-API:  The SP-API provides several endpoints to monitor and adjust pricing . The Pricing
API (for example, getPricing  or getItemOffers ) lets a seller retrieve the current prices for
all offers on an ASIN, including the Buy Box price and lowest prices. Sellers can use this to
algorithmically reprice – if a competitor goes lower , your system can detect it via API and lower
your price (if within your floor) to stay competitive. The SP-API Feeds  allow you to update your
own price programmatically. For measuring impact, a seller can pull order reports or sales
metrics via SP-API. For instance, the Orders API can give you order counts over time (though for
conversion rate, you’d also need session data from a Business Report). Alternatively, the
Business Reports can be accessed via SP-API to track units sold and conversion rate alongside
price changes. Some specific SP-API tools: the Catalog Items API  can return the list price  and
potentially the sales rank, which combined with your own offer price can indicate
competitiveness (if your offer price is above the list/MSRP, sales might suffer). Also, an important
measure is if Amazon flags your price as too high – the SP-API’s Listings API might return a 
pricing status  (e.g., stranded or suppressed listing info if your price violates fair pricing policies).
In summary, sellers measure price impact by tracking sales before and after price adjustments
(manually or through API data) and by watching their Buy Box percentage – a price change often
immediately reflects in Buy Box wins, which is measurable.
(UK specifics:  Remember that UK prices must include VAT. So a price of £20 on Amazon UK is the final
price a buyer pays (assuming free shipping with Prime). Sellers need to account for the ~20% VAT in
their margins. If a small seller is not VAT-registered (under the threshold), they effectively have a 20%
margin advantage and might price slightly lower . However , Amazon since 2021 handles VAT collection
for most transactions to ensure compliance, so all sellers are competing on a level playing field in terms
of the  price seen by the customer . Another UK aspect is currency and local economic conditions – UK
shoppers might be more price-sensitive in certain categories due to readily comparing with high-street
retailer prices that also include VAT. Additionally, during big seasonal events (Black Friday, Boxing Day
sales), price competition intensifies – sellers often lower prices or run deals, which significantly boosts
sales volume in those periods.)
4. Product Ranking (Organic Search Position)

keywords – has a profound effect on sales. Most Amazon purchases begin with a search query, and
products that rank on the first page (especially near the top) get exponentially more visibility (and thus
clicks and sales) than those buried deeper . This ranking is largely a result of Amazon’s algorithm (A9/•

(not to be confused with Best Sellers Rank , which is a sales metric) determines how many shoppers will
find it among search results, regardless of which seller ultimately gets the sale.
Impact on Sales:  A higher (better) organic ranking for popular search terms will dramatically increase
traffic  to the listing, leading to more potential sales. In fact, studies show that the vast majority of
Amazon shoppers do not go beyond the first page of results. Roughly 70-80% of customers stick to
page one , and the top few results capture the most clicks. For example, one analysis found that the
first three organic listings get about 60-64% of clicks . If your product ranks #1 for a high-volume
keyword, it will likely receive a flood of views and consequently sales. Conversely, if it ranks on page 3 or
4, it might get only a trickle of traffic. Being on page one is often essential: more than 80% of Amazon
sales happen on the first page of search results . So, a product that ranks higher will sell more simply
because more people find and consider it. This is a  direct  impact: rank up = sales up. Additionally,
Amazon’s Best Sellers Rank (BSR)  in a category is a reflection of recent sales velocity; while not exactly
the same as search rank, a low BSR (#1 is highest seller) further boosts visibility through the Best Sellers
pages and badges (e.g. a “#1 Best Seller” badge can increase conversion and clicks too). Losing rank
(e.g., due to a period of low sales or going out of stock) can start a vicious cycle: lower rank means fewer
views, which means fewer sales, which further hurts rank. On the other hand, strong sales (from any
seller) improve the product’s rank, bringing in more traffic. So, organic rank is both an outcome of good
sales and a driver of future sales  – it’s a feedback loop.
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Amazon’s search algorithm aims to show customers the products most
likely to satisfy their query and result in a purchase. Factors influencing organic ranking include the
product’s textual relevance (keywords in title, bullets, etc.), sales history, conversion rate, customer
reviews, and stock availability. For buyers, the mechanism is simple: visibility . Shoppers typically type in
a search (e.g. “wireless earbuds”) and then mostly click on the first few listings that catch their eye.
Many will refine by Prime or by rating, but within those filtered results, position matters greatly . Being
the top result with a good title and rating means a large share of shoppers will click your product page.
If your shared listing is the one that ranks high, all sellers on it benefit from the increased traffic
(though who actually gets the sale will depend on the Buy Box). Essentially, products on the first page
serve as the storefront display  – if you’re not there, most buyers won’t even know your item exists. UK
buyers behave similarly to global shoppers in this regard – they rarely scroll through dozens of pages.
Often they’ll consider the top results and maybe use the left-side filters (by brand, rating, Prime, price
range)  to  narrow  down.  If  your  product  is  not  ranked  well  for  important  keywords  (including  UK
spellings or terms if they differ , e.g., “colour” vs “color”), you’ll miss out on a huge chunk of potential
sales. Another aspect in the UK: if your product has a regional appeal or a UK-specific use, you want it to
rank  for  those  local  keywords.  Buyers  also  respond  to  ranking  indirectly  via  the  Best  Seller  and
Amazon’s Choice badges  which are often attached to high-ranking items. A “Best Seller” badge (for #1
in a category) next to your product in search results can further attract clicks, creating a winner-takes-
most situation. In summary, buyer behavior heavily favors what is easily visible : the higher you rank, the
more buyers will click your listing instead of a competitor’s.
Observation/Measurement:  Measuring organic rank can be done by searching for your product’s
keywords or by using analytic tools, as well as monitoring sales rank metrics.
On Amazon’s website:  The straightforward (if manual) way is to search for relevant keywords and
see where the product listing appears in the results. For example, search “gaming headset UK”
and see if your item is on page 1 or 2. This can be time-consuming, and results may be
personalized, so it’s often better to use an incognito browser or a rank-tracking tool. Amazon
itself doesn’t tell sellers their keyword positions, but third-party software (Helium 10, Jungle
Scout, etc.) can track this. Another metric on the site is the Best Sellers Rank (BSR)  displayed on•

the product page in the “Product details” section (for each category the product is listed in). BSR
is an indicator of sales velocity relative to that category. If your BSR improves (closer to #1), it
implies sales have increased; if it worsens (higher number), sales have slowed. A dramatically
improved BSR often correlates with better search positioning due to high recent sales. You can
also observe the presence of badges: if your product gains a “Best Seller” or “Amazon’s Choice”
badge for a keyword, that’s evidence of strong performance/visibility. UK-specific observation:
ensure you search on Amazon.co.uk (as rankings are marketplace-specific) and use UK keywords

Using SP-API:  Amazon’s SP-API does not directly provide keyword search rank  data (Amazon keeps
the search algorithm results proprietary). However , you can infer some aspects: the Catalog
Items API  can return the product’s sales ranks  in various categories (the API includes an 
itemSalesRanks  field). For instance, you can get the BSR for the Electronics category for your
ASIN via API. Tracking BSR over time via the API can show trends – if a product’s BSR goes from
500 to 100, it’s selling more relative to others, likely due to better visibility. Also, the API can
confirm if the product has the “Best Seller” badge in a subcategory by checking if BSR = 1 in that
subcategory. For keyword ranking, sellers often rely on external methods or the Advertising API
(for sponsored rank) – not directly SP-API. Some creative approaches include using the Product
Advertising API  (a different Amazon API) which can return search results for a keyword with
ASINs in order , but the SP-API itself is limited here. Instead, many sellers use third-party services
or scrape the search page (not officially allowed) to gauge their organic positions. Another
measure is to look at the Search Analytics (Brand Analytics)  if you are brand-registered:
Amazon’s Brand Analytics (accessible via API for brand owners) provides data like the top 3
products for a given search term and their share of clicks and conversions. This can help you see
if your product is among the top clicked for that term. In the UK context, one could use the same
API to check search popularity of UK-specific terms and see if their product appears. In
summary, while direct rank isn’t exposed via SP-API, sales rank (BSR)  is, and that’s a good proxy
for overall performance. Monitoring your BSR via API or your conversion rate (since Amazon’s
algorithm takes conversion/sales into account for rank ) can indirectly tell you if your organic
visibility is likely improving.
5. Seasonality and Sales Patterns
Seasonality  refers to the predictable fluctuations in demand for certain products or overall sales due to
the time of year , holidays, or seasonal events. Many products experience peaks and troughs at specific
times  –  for  example,  toys  and  gift  items  surge  in  November/December  (holiday  season),  fitness
products  sell  more  in  January  (New  Year’s  resolutions),  outdoor  furniture  spikes  in  summer ,  etc.
Understanding these patterns is crucial for FBA sellers to manage stock and expectations. Seasonality
indirectly  influences sales: it’s not controlled by sellers, but it dramatically changes the baseline demand.
Impact on Sales:  During peak season for a product, sales can increase massively even without any
changes to price, ranking, or other factors, simply because  more people are buying that type of item .
Conversely, in off-season, even the best-optimized listing may see sluggish sales because customer
interest is low. For instance, Q4 (fourth quarter)  is famously the biggest sales period on Amazon UK, as
it includes Black Friday, Cyber Monday, and Christmas – many sellers see a large fraction of their annual
sales in just November and December . Amazon’s overall traffic and sales volume swell during this
period. Some data indicates Amazon’s Q4 online store sales jump significantly year-over-year due to
holiday shopping. For specific categories: e.g., health supplements often show a bump in January (post-
holiday health kicks); “Valentine’s Day” related products peak in late Jan to early Feb; back-to-school
items surge in late summer . An Amazon analysis notes that seasonal demand patterns repeat fairly
consistently each year . A study of common categories showed, for example:  January = spike in
sports nutrition, Feb = Valentine’s gifts, spring = graduations and Mother’s Day gifts, summer =•

weekends or payday). For UK-specific patterns, one might consider events like  Black Friday/Cyber

restock limits can tighten around holidays). If not prepared, a seller might stock out during a peak,
missing out on a year’s best sales. Conversely, stocking too much for a season that doesn’t pan out can
leave you with excess inventory and storage fees.
Mechanism  (Buyer  Behavior):  Seasonality  reflects  changes  in  buyer  behavior  driven  by  the
calendar . People naturally buy gifts in Q4, fitness gear in January, garden supplies in spring, etc. These
trends are influenced by holidays, weather , and cultural habits. On Amazon, this is seen in search trends
– e.g., searches for “costume” skyrocket in October (Halloween) then plummet. Buyers also respond to
Amazon’s  own  seasonal  promotions:  Prime  Day  (usually  in  July,  and  a  second  one  sometimes  in
October) creates a spike in mid-summer sales across many categories as people hunt for deals. The UK
has its own holiday schedule (e.g., earlier Christmas cut-off shipping dates, Boxing Day sales tradition,
etc.) which can slightly alter when peaks occur (UK consumers might also do a lot of shopping in late
November due to Black Friday, and continue heavy buying until mid-December , then a last-minute spike
just before Amazon’s Christmas delivery cut-off). Seasonal demand means buyers have higher intent
during  peaks  –  conversion  rates  often  rise  because  people  are  actively  looking  to  purchase  (for
example, a gift item in early December might convert very well as people are in gift-buying mode,
whereas in March that same item might sit idle). It also means more competition may enter – sellers
know the season is hot and might list seasonal items, increasing offer counts and possibly leading to
price competition in peak season (or conversely, prices might rise if supply is short relative to demand).
For buyers, seasonal sales events (Black Friday etc.) create expectation of discounts, so many hold off

6. Review Count and Average Rating (Social Proof)
Customer reviews and ratings  on Amazon heavily influence conversion rates and sales. When selling
on a shared listing, all sellers benefit from (or suffer from) the same pool of reviews since the product
reviews are attached to the ASIN, not to individual sellers. Two key aspects matter: the  quantity of
reviews  and the average star rating . These together form the product’s “social proof.” A large number
of positive reviews signals to buyers that the product is popular and reliable, increasing their likelihood
of purchase. Conversely, a low rating or very few reviews can deter buyers.
Impact on Sales:  Strong reviews (both in quality and quantity) can  significantly boost conversion
rates , meaning more of the people who view the listing end up buying. This directly increases sales
volume without needing more traffic. There are some striking statistics:  Products with a substantial
number of reviews convert much better than those with only a handful.  For example, one analysis of
Amazon  data  showed  products  with  50+  reviews  see  conversion  rates  2-3  times  higher  than
products with under 10 reviews . This is a massive difference – going from 5 reviews to 50 reviews• 
9
could potentially triple your sales conversion, purely due to buyer trust. Additionally, having a high
average star rating dramatically affects buyer behavior . A study by Pattern found that for each 1-star
increase in rating, conversion rate increases by roughly 4-5% , so improving from a 3-star average to a 5-
star average raised conversion by about 12%  in their analysis. In practical terms, a product rated 4.5★
will far outsell an otherwise identical product rated 3.5★, especially in a competitive category. Products
below ~4.0 stars tend to see steep drops in conversion as many Amazon shoppers filter out or avoid
lower-rated items. A high review count  also improves click-through rate in search – Amazon’s internal
data suggests listings with robust review numbers get  15-25% higher click-through from search
results  (likely because of the eye-catching high rating and count). On the flip side, negative reviews or a
low rating can tank sales: if the product has poor reviews, all sellers on that listing will struggle to sell it,
no matter their price or Buy Box status, because customers may simply not want it. Reviews also have
an indirect impact on  search ranking  – Amazon’s algorithm factors in rating and review volume as
signals of product quality and popularity. So good reviews can help maintain or improve organic rank,
further boosting sales. In summary, more good reviews = more trust = more sales; poor or few reviews
= friction in the purchase decision = fewer sales.
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Reviews serve as social proof and quality assurance  for buyers. When
a shopper lands on a product page, one of the first things they notice is the star rating and the number
of  reviews.  Many  buyers  will  read  at  least  a  few  reviews  (especially  the  negative  ones)  before
purchasing. If the product has hundreds of reviews and a ~4.5★ average, a buyer feels confident that
“many others bought and liked this, so it’s probably a safe buy.” If there are only 2 reviews, or the rating
is 3★, the buyer hesitates – they may think the product is unproven or unreliable. Often, shoppers will
compare similar items by rating: for instance, a buyer might choose a slightly more expensive item with
4.3★ rating over a cheaper one with 3.8★ because they trust the former’s quality more.  Threshold
effects  exist too: crossing the 4★ average threshold is crucial, as many customers filter search results
by “4 stars & up.” If a product falls below 4.0, it might literally disappear from consideration for those
users. Likewise, having at least a certain number of reviews (often cited as 10 or more) provides a
baseline credibility – fewer than that, and buyers might feel there isn’t enough information about the
product’s  performance.  UK  buyers  are  similar  to  global  buyers  in  this  regard  –  they  place  a  high
importance on reviews. In fact, UK consumers often specifically read negative reviews to see what
issues might exist. Reviews about a UK-specific aspect (say, a UK electrical plug compliance, or UK sizing
for apparel) can be particularly influential. Another behavioral point: products with a high volume of
reviews often get additional labels like “#1 Best Seller” (if applicable) or show up in “Customers also
bought”  widgets  more  frequently,  reinforcing  their  popularity.  Also,  Amazon’s  “Amazon’s  Choice”
badge often attaches to listings with a good combination of high rating and decent price for a given
keyword, further swaying buyers. The presence of abundant reviews can overcome other hesitations –
for example, even if multiple sellers are on the listing, the buyer trusts the product itself due to reviews,
so competition then is just on price/Prime. Reviews apply to all sellers equally (except seller feedback,
which is separate but less visible), so your main concern as a shared listing seller is the product’s
collective review profile. If one bad seller causes a spate of negative product reviews (e.g., by sending
faulty units), it hurts everyone’s conversion. Conversely, if the brand or community efforts drive more
positive reviews, everyone’s sales benefit.

7. Listing Quality (Content: Titles, Bullet Points, Images, etc.)
The quality of the product listing content – including the title, images, bullet points, description, and
overall accuracy – plays a crucial role in both attracting buyers (driving clicks) and converting them once
on the page. A well-optimized, informative, and professional-looking listing will instill confidence and
answer customer questions, leading to higher conversion rates and better search visibility. In a shared
listing scenario, typically the content is shared among sellers (usually controlled by the brand owner or
the first lister). All sellers benefit from good content (it helps sell the product) or suffer from bad content
(it can confuse or deter customers).
Impact  on  Sales:  High-quality  listing  content  improves  conversion  and  sales  by  presenting  the

High-resolution, clear images (and plenty of them) can dramatically increase conversion rates .

and  make  the  product  more  enticing,  whereas  a  listing  with  only  one  low-quality  image  will  turn
customers away. Amazon has even stated that products with poor or insufficient images tend to have
lower conversion and may lose relevance in search results  (because they don’t sell as well). Bullet
points and descriptions that  highlight key benefits  and features will also boost sales by convincing
customers the product meets their needs. If a listing clearly communicates the value (e.g., through
benefit-driven bullet points) and addresses common questions or concerns, buyers are more likely to
hit “Buy”. Conversely, a poorly written listing – with a vague title, missing info, spelling errors, or just a
couple of bullet points – undermines trust and can cause potential buyers to leave (perhaps to find a
listing that gives them more confidence). Good content also includes relevant keywords which help the
product  appear  in  more  searches  (thus  indirectly  boosting  sales  via  more  traffic).  For  instance,  a
thorough title and bullet points improve your search indexing. Moreover , the presence of enhanced
content  like  A+  Content  (for  brand  owners)  can  further  improve  conversion  by  providing  richer
information. While A+ Content isn’t indexed for search, it can increase sales by demonstrating quality

few percentage points or more, which for an Amazon listing can translate to significant sales gains. For

12-15%, which is a 20-50% increase in sales per the same traffic.
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Buyers make snap judgments based on the listing. When browsing

then details, shoppers get their questions answered and feel the product suits them. If important info is
missing or buried, the buyer might get frustrated or unsure and click away. Also, a wall of text or too-

focus on customer benefits. A good description  or A+ content can further reassure buyers by telling the
brand story, providing comparison charts or detailed diagrams. All these elements together reduce the
buyer’s uncertainty and increase trust that the product will meet their needs, thereby increasing the
likelihood of purchase. Another factor is professionalism: a well-formatted, typo-free listing with crisp
images signals that the product is sold by competent, quality-focused sellers (and likely that the product

8. Stock Availability and Sales Velocity
Inventory availability – whether the product is in stock, and how much stock is available – has a direct
impact  on  sales.  Simply  put,  you  can’t  sell  what  you  don’t  have  in  stock.  For  shared  listings,  stock
availability can refer to two things: the product’s overall availability on Amazon  (if all sellers are out
of stock, the listing can’t make sales), and each individual seller’s stock  (if you run out, you lose the
ability to make sales until you restock, even if others continue selling). Sales velocity is closely tied to
staying in stock; consistent availability allows continuous sales and helps maintain your sales rank and
Buy Box status.
Impact on Sales:  If a product goes out of stock , its sales drop to zero for that period – it’s the most
immediate and absolute impact. For a single seller  listing (like a private label product), running out of
FBA stock means the listing effectively becomes unavailable (unless you have an FBM backup offer), and
sales halt. This not only pauses revenue but can also hurt the product’s momentum and ranking.
Amazon’s algorithm penalizes stockouts because no sales are happening (and customers can’t buy the
item). When you eventually restock, you might find that your search rank fell due to the inactivity, and it
can  take  time  (and  possibly  effort  like  ads  or  promotions)  to  climb  back  up.  For  shared  listings
(wholesale) , the dynamic is a bit different: if  you stock out but other sellers still have inventory, the
listing continues to sell through those other sellers . You, however , lose your share of sales entirely
while you’re out. The listing’s overall sales velocity might remain the same (so its rank in Amazon’s eyes
doesn’t suffer), but your  seller account registers zero sales for that ASIN until you restock. Essentially,
your competitors seize your portion of the sales. For example, imagine 5 sellers were each getting 20
sales a day on a listing (100/day total). If one seller runs out, the product likely still sells ~100/day (the
demand is still there), but now divided among 4 sellers – each might get 25/day or one of them picks up
the extra depending on Buy Box rotation. That one seller who ran out has gone from 20 sales/day to 0 –
a huge impact – but the product’s  total  sales stayed constant, meaning the ASIN’s rank and overall2
14
performance  didn’t  drop.  So  being  out  of  stock  is  essentially  losing  potential  sales  and  allowing
competitors to take your sales. Another aspect is the Buy Box impact : if your stock is very low, Amazon
might  rotate  you  out  of  the  Buy  Box  more  often  to  avoid  customer  orders  that  can’t  be  fulfilled
(especially if your inventory hits 0 and you haven’t updated it yet, you’ll lose Buy Box immediately).
Staying well-stocked can improve your Buy Box stability, because Amazon prefers offers that won’t run
out. Also, stock levels can influence winning of the Buy Box indirectly; if two sellers are equal but one
has only 1 unit left and the other has 50 units, Amazon might favor the one with deeper stock (to ensure
continuity). There’s a concept of sales velocity vs inventory : if an item is selling extremely fast, you risk
stocking out – some sellers will raise price or even intentionally slow sales (e.g., disable ads) to avoid
going out of stock, because a stockout’s negative effects on ranking can outweigh the temporary profit
of selling those last units quickly. In summary, being consistently in stock enables consistent sales. Each
stockout is essentially missed revenue and can break the sales momentum of a product. Conversely,
having inventory ready for seasonal spikes or sudden surges means you can capture all possible sales.
In FBA, another factor is inventory placement : if your stock is stored only in one FC and it runs out there
(even if you have stock elsewhere or inbound), some regional Prime delivery promises might suffer –
Amazon now tries to redistribute, but in the UK if you have inventory in only one warehouse and it all
sells out in the south, northern customers might see delays or even the offer temporarily not Prime-
eligible in certain postcodes. That’s a nuance of regional availability affecting sales.
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Buyers generally won’t know or care about stock levels until it hits “out
of stock.” When an item is in stock, Amazon might show “Only X left in stock – order soon” to create
urgency, but if it actually hits zero, the listing will either show “Currently unavailable”  (if no other sellers
have it) or simply no longer show that seller’s offer . If the whole listing is out of stock (no seller can fulfill
it), buyers cannot purchase – often they might click “Notify me when available” or just look for an
alternative product. Many buyers on Amazon will choose a similar product from another brand if one is
unavailable. So a stockout can result not just in delayed sales but permanently lost sales  to competitors
(the customer might not wait; they buy a different item). If some sellers are still in stock, the buyer
seamlessly buys from whoever is available – possibly at a higher price if the in-stock sellers are priced
higher (which can in the short term raise the ASP, but long term it might annoy customers if price jumps
too high). Frequent stock issues can also damage buyer trust or satisfaction – if a listing is often sold
out, customers might consider it unreliable. On the other hand, as long as  someone  has stock, the
average buyer just sees the product is available. They might not even notice if the specific seller they
bought from last time is now gone; they only care that the product “ships now.” For FBA, Amazon will
generally continue the Prime 1-day promise as long as any FBA seller has stock in the UK. For individual
sellers, one subtle buyer-behavior aspect is that if you run out and your offer disappears, any repeat
customers  or  people  who  specifically  wanted  to  buy  from  you  (maybe  you  offered  a  bundle  or
something unique) will be unable to. But that’s a niche case – generally, on shared listings, buyers care
more about the product than the seller . So your stockout mostly benefits the other sellers, as buyers
just shift to them. This is why some wholesale sellers actually prefer to let themselves stock out rather
than overstock, because they know the listing’s sales velocity won’t drop – they can jump back in later
without the listing losing rank. They’d rather free up cash for other products in the meantime. In
contrast, a private label seller losing the Buy Box to nobody  (i.e., going out of stock entirely) loses sales
to competing products in the market; repeat customers might not find it, etc. In summary, buyers will
buy what’s available – stock availability ensures your  offer is available to fulfill that demand.
Observation/Measurement:  Keeping an eye on inventory levels and how they correlate with sales is
key. Amazon provides tools to monitor stock and even to predict when you might run out.
On Amazon’s site:  When viewing the product page as a seller or buyer , if stock is low Amazon will
sometimes show “Only 3 left in stock” etc., which is a cue about availability. If you see “Currently
unavailable” on a listing, it means no stock at all (and thus no sales are happening). Sellers• 
15
should monitor their in-stock rate  – Amazon has an In-Stock Rate metric (especially for FBM, but
for FBA it’s more about managing your FBA inventory through the Restock report). On the
product page, if multiple sellers exist, you can click the other sellers and see their stock
(sometimes by attempting to add a high quantity to cart to see the limit, though Amazon often
caps this info at 10+). For a more analytical approach, third-party tools like Keepa also track when
a listing goes out of stock by noting gaps in the Buy Box or price history. Keepa graphs can even
show the new offer count over time; a sudden drop to 0 sellers or 0 stock is visible. Additionally,
you can glean stock availability by sales rank patterns: if a product’s BSR suddenly gets much
worse (number goes up dramatically) and no other reason (like a price hike) is obvious, a
stockout might have occurred causing sales to drop. 
Using SP-API:  The SP-API has multiple endpoints to manage and monitor inventory. For your own
stock, the Inventory API  (getInventorySummaries  or older FBA Inventory Report ) will
tell you how many units you have available at FBA, how many inbound, etc. You can set up alerts
when stock falls below a threshold. The Reports API  can fetch the Inventory Health  or Restock
Inventory  reports which include data on days of supply left. To measure impact on sales velocity,
you can use the Orders API  or Sales reports  to see units sold per day and cross-reference with
inventory events (e.g., did sales drop to 0 on a date you ran out?). You might also use the Feeds
API to update inventory for FBM listings and ensure you never oversell (overselling leads to
cancellations which hurt metrics). For competitive analysis, while you cannot get other sellers’
stock via API directly, you can use the Products Pricing API  to see if Amazon considers the
listing in stock and the Buy Box price (if no price, maybe buy box suppressed or out of stock
scenario). Some advanced sellers use the MWS API (old) or SC Buy Box reports to detect when
their offer lost the buy box (which can be due to stockout or pricing). Importantly, Amazon’s SP-
API doesn’t explicitly say “listing is out of stock” because if another seller has it, the listing is still
active. But you can infer by OfferCount : if you query an ASIN’s offer count and it’s 0, then
nobody has stock (or listing is blocked). If you query your own offer through the Listings API and
it returns inactive (zero quantity), you know you’re out. Another measurement is Lost Sales  due
to stockouts – some tools compute this by looking at the sales run-rate before stockout and how
long you were out. You can do this manually by extracting order history: e.g., if you normally sold
5/day and were out for 10 days, you roughly lost 50 sales. SP-API gives you the data to calculate
that. Also, sellers can track in-stock rate  via the FBA Inventory Dashboard  metrics (which might
be available via API as a report). In summary, staying in stock is measured simply by tracking
inventory and sales continuity. For an FBA seller in the UK, also watch Amazon’s restock limits
(via SP-API or reports) – if you can’t send enough stock, you might run out in peak season,
affecting sales.
(UK specifics:  After Brexit, inventory management for UK versus EU became separate. Many sellers had
to stock FBA inventory in the UK fulfillment centers exclusively for UK orders. Remote Fulfillment (EFN)
from EU to UK  is an option but comes with slower delivery and often the offers appear as non-Prime
with longer shipping  or with import fees, which is very detrimental to sales – thus, serious sellers will

It’s crucial to monitor that UK stock – you can’t lean on EU stock to back you up quickly due to customs
delays. Regional distribution in UK: Amazon UK has fulfillment centers across the country; having your
inventory spread can help ensure one-day delivery promise nationwide. Amazon largely handles that,
but if stock is low, it might all be in one FC, and customers far away might see 2-day delivery, which
could  reduce  conversions  in  those  areas.  Also,  UK  has  seasonal  spikes  (e.g.,  end  of  year)  where
stockouts are common  if planning isn’t accurate – running out during Christmas means losing the
year’s best opportunity. Another UK point:  storage fees  in UK (especially Q4) are high; some sellers
under-stock to avoid fees but risk stockouts – a balance is needed. With the SP-API’s help, sellers might
use tools to forecast UK demand specifically (since you can’t easily transfer EU stock to UK quickly
anymore). Additionally, consider FBA cut-off dates : Amazon UK usually sets a latest check-in date for• 
16
Christmas inventory. If you miss it and stock arrives late, you might as well be out of stock for that peak.
So sales could drop to zero even if you technically have stock (just not in FBA in time). To UK buyers, if
something is out of stock or has long delivery, they often just pick a different Prime item. So it’s vital to
keep healthy stock levels in UK FBA for consistent sales velocity. Finally, sellers can enroll in programs
like FBA Backorder  or Allow customers to order when out of stock  (not common on Amazon except
for Amazon Retail or specific situations) – generally, Amazon doesn’t take backorders for 3P sellers; the
listing just goes unavailable. So avoiding that scenario is critical.)
9. Fulfillment Metrics: Delivery Speed and Prime Eligibility
The method of fulfillment – particularly whether an offer is Prime-eligible and how fast the delivery
promise is – strongly influences both the Buy Box and customer purchase decisions. Prime eligibility
(achieved via FBA or Seller Fulfilled Prime) is crucial on Amazon UK, as Prime members tend to prefer
Prime offers for the fast, free shipping. Delivery time  (the estimated arrival date shown) can make or
break a sale: many customers will choose a slightly more expensive or lesser-known seller if it means
they get the product tomorrow instead of next week. Fulfillment-related metrics also include things like
on-time shipment rate, order defect rate, etc., but those mostly influence whether you can be Prime or
win the Buy Box at all (poor metrics can exclude you from Buy Box).
Impact on Sales:  A Prime, fast-shipping offer will typically outsell  a non-Prime or slow-shipping offer
by a huge margin. Indeed, all else equal, Amazon gives the Buy Box to the Prime offer the vast majority
of the time. If you are an FBA seller , you automatically get the Prime badge and Amazon’s reliable
delivery. This attracts more buyers and thus more sales. If you are not FBA (Fulfilled by Merchant)
without SFP, your offer might be at a disadvantage: even if you list a slightly lower price, you might still
lose the Buy Box to an FBA seller with Prime because many customers value speed and guaranteed
service over saving a small amount. Amazon knows this and factors it in – fulfillment method is a key
Buy Box factor  (FBA usually “wins” over equivalent FBM). For example, an FBA item priced at £20 might
win over an FBM item at £19 if the FBM has slow shipping or no Prime. Sales volume is boosted by
Prime in part because Prime members are heavy shoppers – they tend to convert more (one stat
indicates Prime members have a much higher conversion rate, possibly because of the free 1-day
shipping) . Anecdotally, the Prime badge can increase conversion rates significantly (some sources
say by up to 20-30% or more) because of the trust and speed . If your offer is not Prime, a large
segment of customers might not even consider it – many filter for Prime-only products. Similarly, faster
delivery promises (e.g., “Get it by tomorrow”) create urgency and convenience, raising the chance of a
sale, whereas an “estimated delivery in 5-7 days” can lead the customer to look for alternatives. In the
UK, Amazon offers very quick delivery options (one-day or even same-day in some areas for Prime). If
your product via FBA can be delivered next day nationwide, you capture those impatient buyers. If
another seller is FBM with a 3-5 day delivery, they will lose out on those sales. Seller metrics  like late
shipment rate and cancellation rate indirectly impact sales by affecting Buy Box eligibility – if those
metrics slip, Amazon may remove you from Buy Box contention until you improve. But assuming you
meet the thresholds, the main differentiator is still whether you have that Prime tag and how fast.
Overall,  being  FBA/Prime  gives  a  significant  sales  boost  due  to  both  algorithmic  preference  and
consumer preference. 
Mechanism (Buyer Behavior):  Modern e-commerce shoppers (especially Prime members) expect fast,
reliable delivery. On Amazon’s product page, right below the price, it clearly shows the expected delivery
date and whether it ships with Prime. Buyers often make decisions based on this: if they need an item
soon, they’ll choose the option that arrives fastest. Many will even pay a bit more for faster delivery. The
Prime badge  is also a proxy for trust – it implies Amazon’s customer service and easy returns, which
customers appreciate. In the UK, Prime membership is widespread and includes benefits like free One-
Day Delivery, so Prime members usually filter  searches by Prime eligible only. If your offer isn’t Prime, a3

huge chunk of customers might not even see it in their filtered results. Even those who do see it might
be skeptical or reluctant (non-Prime implies shipping costs and slower delivery, and possibly less trust in
the  seller’s  fulfillment).  Buyer  behavior  clearly  shows  preference:  one  study  noted  that  the  Prime
checkmark  drastically  increases  conversion,  especially  on  mobile .  People  have  come  to
associate Prime with a good shopping experience. If two offers are on the same listing – one Prime, one
not – the Prime one will get the Buy Box and hence nearly all the sales unless it’s dramatically more
expensive. Delivery time matters too: sometimes two Prime offers might differ – one might say “Get it

purchases (gifts needed by a certain date, or consumables needed soon), speed is key. Another factor:
Fulfillment  by  Amazon  versus  seller  fulfillment  affects  buyer  confidence  in  customer  service  and
return handling. Many buyers know that with FBA, Amazon will handle any issues, whereas with an
unknown FBM seller , returns might be a hassle. So they choose FBA offers because they feel “safer ,”

expectations further . A non-Prime offer taking a week from, say, overseas is at a huge disadvantage.
Therefore, buyer behavior heavily favors sellers who can offer  Fulfilled by Amazon (Prime)  and fast
delivery. This factor can override others: some buyers will take a Prime offer even if it has slightly fewer
reviews or a slightly higher price because they want the quick, guaranteed delivery.
Observation/Measurement:  Fulfillment metrics can be observed both in how Amazon displays offers
and through seller performance dashboards.
On Amazon’s site:  The simplest way is to look at the offer on the product page: does it have the 
Prime logo ? And what delivery date is shown (“Get it by Tuesday, Nov 25 with Prime”). You can
also check other sellers: perhaps a non-Prime seller is offering a lower price – see if Amazon still
shows them only under “Other sellers” due to lack of Prime. That contrast tells you how strong
the Prime advantage is (if a non-Prime needs to be much cheaper to even be considered).
Furthermore, check your Seller Central Account Health  and Performance metrics : these show
your order defect rate, late shipment rate, valid tracking rate, etc. If those are all green/good,
you are eligible for Prime/Buy Box (assuming you use FBA or have SFP). If any are in warning,
your ability to compete might drop. The Buy Box statistics also sometimes indicate if your non-
Prime offer is being skipped. As a measurement, you might do a test: list one SKU as FBA and
one as FBM and see the sales difference – often the FBA one will sell far more. For UK specifically,
consider regional delivery : try inputting different UK postcodes on the product page to see if
delivery date changes or if certain sellers disappear for far-off regions (a sign of regional
fulfillment effects). Amazon’s default is to show fastest offer for the shopper’s location. If you
notice your offer isn’t appearing when you change postcode, it might be because your stock isn’t
in a nearby FC. That suggests you might need to send inventory to more distribution centers
(Amazon usually handles distribution if you send enough stock).
